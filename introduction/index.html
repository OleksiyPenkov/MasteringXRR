Front Matter

Introduction

About This Book

This book shows you how to fit an X-ray reflectivity (XRR) curve. You start with a curve measured on your sample. At the end you have the thickness, roughness and density of each layer. You also have a report that another person can check.

All the work is done in one program, X-Ray Calc 3. Every step in the book is something you do in its window: a menu command, a dialog or a field you fill in. The book explains the physics you need for the next step in the program, and no more.

The steps come from a written fitting procedure of 13 steps (Penkov, Peng and Fu, 2026). It records how the author fits a curve in X-Ray Calc, with the reason for each step. It agrees with the author’s practice in 51 saved projects. Seven of its choices were tested against curves calculated from a known structure. Three failed and were replaced: the choices for the scale, for smoothing and for the end of the fitting range. The book teaches the tested version. Chapter 14: Four settings, in this order explains the three replacements.

The book has five Parts:

  • Part 1, Getting Started, says what fitting can tell you, introduces the program and takes you through a first quick fit.
  • Part 2, The Physics You Need, and No More, explains how X-rays reflect from thin films. It also explains how X-Ray Calc calculates a curve and how it searches for a fit.
  • Part 3, The Fitting Workflow, Step by Step, is the procedure itself, in the order you do it.
  • Part 4, Special Cases and Reporting, covers density, single films, graded multilayers and the final report.
  • Part 5, The Part of Tens, gives short lists to check a fit against.

Conventions used in this book

  • Menu commands are in bold, with > between the levels. Data > Normalize (Auto) means: open the Data menu and choose Normalize (Auto). Buttons, dialogs and fields are also in bold, spelled as the program shows them.
  • Every length is in ångströms (Å), including roughness and wavelength. Density is in g/cm³, and angles are in degrees. These are the program’s own units, so the numbers in the book match the numbers on your screen.
  • Every angle is labeled. The program’s chart shows the angle 2θ. The physics uses θ, the angle between the beam and the surface. So the book writes 2θ = 1.2° or θ = 0.6°, never a bare 0.6°. Chapter 2: 2θ or θ: the angle on your screen shows where the program sets the angle it displays.
  • Numbers from the author’s laboratory are marked as such. Each one is an example from one instrument. Your instrument needs its own value.

Each chapter works on its own. When a chapter needs an earlier one, it names it in one sentence with a link.

The book has a web edition and a PDF edition with the same text.

Foolish Assumptions

The book assumes a few things about you:

  • You have a measured XRR curve, or you’ll have one soon.
  • You know what a thin film is.
  • You have never fitted an XRR curve.
  • You haven’t taken a course in X-ray optics. Part 2 gives you the physics you need.
  • You have a PC with 64-bit Windows 10 or later.

You don’t need to program. You also don’t need to know how the fitting algorithm works inside. Chapter 9: How X-Ray Calc Finds a Fit explains the part of it you need.

Beyond the Book

X-Ray Calc has its own manual. It describes every dialog, including features this book doesn’t use.

In X-Ray Calc

Help > User Manual opens the program’s manual. The shortcut is F1.

The X-Ray Calc wiki covers the same ground for new users: getting started, manual fitting and working with measured data. It is at github.com/OleksiyPenkov/X-RayCalc3/wiki.

X-Ray Calc installs with demonstration projects in its Examples folder. Chapter 2: Opening a demo project opens one of them. The worked examples in this book use measured curves. They come with the book as X-Ray Calc projects (.xrcx files).

The web edition has figures that you can change. In Chapter 13: The Sensitivity Check, for example, a slider changes one parameter of a model, and you see which feature of the curve moves. The PDF shows the same figures as fixed panels.

Chapter 25: Ten Checks Before You Trust a Number is a one-page checklist. Print it and keep it next to the computer you fit on.

If you publish a result from X-Ray Calc, cite the two papers that describe the program: Penkov et al. (2020) and Penkov et al. (2024). The full references are in the Sources section.

Where to Go from Here

  • XRR is new to you: start with Chapter 1: What XRR Fitting Is, and What It Can Tell You. Then read Chapter 2: Meet X-Ray Calc 3 and Chapter 3: Your First Fit, the Short Way.
  • You have used X-Ray Calc to calculate curves: start with Chapter 3: Your First Fit, the Short Way. It shows a whole fit and names the chapter that explains each step.
  • You know the physics of X-ray reflection: skip Part 2 and start with Chapter 10: Is This Curve Worth Fitting? Every chapter in Part 3 names the section of Part 2 it relies on.
  • You have a fit and you’re not sure it is right: read Chapter 18: Judging a Fit, and then work through the checklist in Chapter 25: Ten Checks Before You Trust a Number.

For the whole workflow on one page, see Chapter 1: The whole workflow on one page.
