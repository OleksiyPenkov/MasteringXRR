A single film on a substrate gives the simplest XRR curve that carries a thickness. Above the critical edge the curve oscillates. These oscillations are Kiessig fringes. Their spacing gives the thickness of the film. Their depth depends on the density and on the roughness. This chapter uses the refractive index n = 1 − δ + iβ and the critical angle from <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch4-x-rays-meet-a-surface#the-critical-angle-and-the-plateau`}>Chapter 4: The critical angle and the plateau</a>.

## Two reflections, one film

A film has two boundaries: its top surface and the interface with the substrate. Part of the beam reflects at the top surface. The rest enters the film, reflects at the interface, and leaves the film again (<a href="#fig-5-1">Figure 5-1</a>). Both reflected waves travel in the same direction and interfere. The second wave has traveled through the film twice, so it lags behind the first. This lag grows with the thickness H and with the angle.

When the lag is a whole number of wavelengths, the two waves add, and the curve has a maximum. As the angle grows, the lag grows. The next maximum comes when the lag has grown by one more wavelength. So, well above the critical angle, the maxima are evenly spaced:

$$
\Delta\theta \approx \frac{\lambda}{2H}
$$

with Δθ in radians. On the X-Ray Calc chart, which shows 2θ, the spacing is Δ(2θ) ≈ λ/H. For a 200 Å film at λ = 1.5406 Å, Δ(2θ) = 1.5406/200 = 0.0077 rad = 0.44°.

<Remember>A thicker film gives more closely spaced fringes. Doubling the thickness halves the spacing.</Remember>

A multilayer also has a top and a bottom, so it shows Kiessig fringes of its whole thickness. Chapter 1 showed them between the Bragg orders of a Co/C multilayer (<a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch1-what-xrr-fitting-is#fig-1-2`}>Chapter 1: Figure 1-2</a>). <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch6-many-films-bragg-peaks#a-stack-that-repeats`}>Chapter 6: A stack that repeats</a> adds the Bragg orders.

<GoingDeeper title="the reflection of one film">

A wave that crosses the film once gains the phase $\varphi = \tfrac{1}{2} q_f H$. Here $q_f = 4\pi \sin\theta_t/\lambda$ is the wave-vector transfer inside the film, with $\theta_t$ the angle of the beam in the film. Let $r_t$ be the reflection coefficient of the top surface and $r_b$ that of the interface with the substrate. The film then reflects

$$
r = \frac{r_t + r_b\,e^{2i\varphi}}{1 + r_t\,r_b\,e^{2i\varphi}}
$$

The term $e^{2i\varphi}$ turns once around the circle each time $q_f H$ grows by 2π. This gives the fringes. How deep they are depends on how $r_b$ compares with $r_t$. If the substrate is replaced by a whole structure, $r_b$ becomes the reflection of that structure. Applied from the substrate upward, one layer at a time, the formula gives the reflection of any stack. X-Ray Calc calculates every curve this way, as <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch8-how-x-ray-calc-computes-a-curve#from-the-substrate-up-the-recursion`}>Chapter 8: From the substrate up: the recursion</a> explains.

</GoingDeeper>

## Reading a thickness from the fringes

<a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a> is a measured curve of a single carbon film on glass. It shows seven fringes above the critical edge. <a href="#table-5-1">Table 5-1</a> lists their positions. The spacing is not constant. It grows from 0.243° between the first two maxima to about 0.27° further out.

The spacing is smaller near the edge because of refraction. Inside the film the beam travels at a smaller angle than outside (<a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch4-x-rays-meet-a-surface#a-refractive-index-just-below-1`}>Chapter 4: A refractive index just below 1</a>). The lag follows the angle inside. So the fringes are evenly spaced in the angle inside the film. Close to the critical angle the two angles differ most. Far above it they are almost equal, and the spacing approaches λ/2H.

The first pair of maxima gives 363 Å. The pairs further out give 320–334 Å. A fit of all seven positions with the refraction included gives 316.8 Å (see the Going Deeper box). A full fit of this curve put 300.5 Å of carbon under a contamination layer 13 Å thick. Together that is 313.5 Å. The fringes measure everything that lies on the substrate, the contamination layer included.

<Warning title="a thickness read from the first fringes">Don't calculate the thickness from the fringes next to the critical edge. There refraction makes the spacing too small. The thickness then comes out too large: 363 Å instead of about 314 Å for the film of <a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a>. Use a pair far above the edge, or count several fringes there and divide.</Warning>

<GoingDeeper title="the fringe positions with refraction">

Inside the film, $\theta_t^2 \approx \theta^2 - 2\delta$, where δ is that of the film. The maxima are evenly spaced in $\theta_t$, by λ/2H. So the j-th maximum lies at

$$
\theta_j^2 \approx \left(\frac{\lambda}{2H}\right)^2 (j + m_0)^2 + 2\delta
$$

with θ in radians. $m_0$ is an unknown offset, because the first fringe you see need not be the first in the count. A least-squares fit of the seven positions of <a href="#table-5-1">Table 5-1</a> for H, $m_0$ and δ gives H = 316.8 Å. The Bragg law with refraction in <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch6-many-films-bragg-peaks#the-bragg-law-with-refraction`}>Chapter 6: The Bragg law with refraction</a> has the same form.

</GoingDeeper>

<InXRayCalc>Move the cursor over a fringe maximum. The Chart Info bar shows its position as **X**, in 2θ by default, and the value as **Y**. To zoom, drag a box around one fringe. **Xmax** and **Rmax** then show the highest point inside the zoomed range. They read the calculated curve of the active model. For a measured curve, read **X** with the cursor. Double-click the chart to reset the zoom.</InXRayCalc>

A fringe needs enough points to be seen. With fewer than two points per fringe, the measured points skip across the fringes. The fringe heights can then seem to rise and fall slowly, which the film does not cause. The curve of <a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a> has a step of 0.003° in 2θ, about 90 points per fringe. **Data > Assess XRR quality ...** checks the points per fringe of a measurement. Fringes closer together than the resolution of the instrument are blurred away before they are counted. For them the check judges the step against the resolution instead (<a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch10-is-this-curve-worth-fitting#when-not-to-fit`}>Chapter 10: When not to fit</a>).

## What density does to the fringes

The depth of the fringes depends on how strongly each boundary reflects. The top surface reflects like the bare film material. The buried interface reflects according to the difference between film and substrate. Well above the critical angle, the reflectivity of a boundary grows with $(\delta_2-\delta_1)^2 + (\beta_2-\beta_1)^2$. The indices 1 and 2 stand for the two materials. When film and substrate have nearly the same δ and β, the interface hardly reflects. The fringes are then weak.

Carbon on silicon is such a case. At its bulk density of 2.266 g/cm³, carbon has δ = 7.28 × 10⁻⁶. Silicon has 7.61 × 10⁻⁶ (<a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch4-x-rays-meet-a-surface#table-4-1`}>Chapter 4: Table 4-1</a>). At 1.8 g/cm³ the δ of carbon falls in proportion, to 5.78 × 10⁻⁶. The interface then reflects about 25 times more strongly. <a href="#fig-5-3">Figure 5-3</a> shows the result for a 200 Å film. At bulk density the fringes are faint: each maximum is less than twice its neighboring minimum. At 1.8 g/cm³ the ratio is 4 to 11. A 200 Å molybdenum film on silicon has a ratio of 22 to 36.

<Figure id="fig-5-3" number="5-3" src={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/figures/fig-5-3-density.svg`} alt="Calculated reflectivity of 200 angstrom films on silicon: molybdenum with deep fringes, carbon at 1.8 grams per cubic centimeter with clear fringes, and carbon at bulk density with faint fringes" caption="Calculated reflectivity of three 200 Å films on Si at λ = 1.5406 Å, all smooth. Carbon at its bulk density, 2.266 g/cm³, is optically close to silicon. Its fringes are faint. At 1.8 g/cm³ they are clear. The dense Mo film has deep fringes. It keeps a high reflectivity up to its own critical angle, at 2θ ≈ 0.87°. Calculated by X-Ray Calc 3.9.4.1250 with no resolution blur." />

A dense film also moves the critical edge. The 200 Å Mo film in <a href="#fig-5-3">Figure 5-3</a> reflects 74 % of the beam at θ = 0.40° and 40 % at θ = 0.45°. Its edge sits at the critical angle of Mo, 0.434°, well above the 0.224° of the silicon under it.

<Remember>A film that is optically close to its substrate shows weak fringes. Its thickness is then hard to read. Its density is harder still.</Remember>

## What roughness does to the fringes

A film has two roughnesses: that of its top surface and that of the interface with the substrate. In X-Ray Calc the σ of a layer is the roughness of its upper interface. So the σ field of the film is the roughness of the top surface. The σ field of the substrate is the roughness of the buried interface.

Roughness weakens the reflection of a boundary. It weakens it more at larger angles. The fringes are deepest when the two boundaries reflect equally. When one of them stops reflecting, the fringes fade. <a href="#fig-5-4">Figure 5-4</a> shows both cases for a 200 Å Mo film on Si:

- **σ = 5 Å at the interface.** The fringes fade steadily with angle. The maximum-to-minimum ratio falls from 16 at θ = 0.53° to 1.7 at θ = 2.78°. The smooth film has 22 and 36 at the same fringes. The mean level of the curve hardly changes.
- **σ = 5 Å on the top surface.** The whole curve falls faster. Between θ = 2° and 2.8° its mean level is half that of the smooth film. The fringes first become deeper, with a ratio of 472 at θ = 1.08°. Then they fade, to 2.5 at θ = 2.79°. The smooth Mo surface reflects more strongly than the Mo/Si interface. A rough top first balances the two reflections. At larger angles it makes the top the weaker one.

<Figure id="fig-5-4" number="5-4" src={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/figures/fig-5-4-roughness.svg`} alt="Calculated reflectivity of a 200 angstrom molybdenum film on silicon: smooth, with 5 angstrom roughness on top, and with 5 angstrom roughness at the interface" caption="Calculated reflectivity of a 200 Å Mo film on Si at λ = 1.5406 Å. Gray: both boundaries smooth. Orange: σ = 5 Å on the top surface (the σ of the film). Blue: σ = 5 Å at the film/substrate interface (the σ of the substrate). Roughness at the interface flattens the fringes. Roughness on the top lowers the whole curve. Calculated by X-Ray Calc 3.9.4.1250 with no resolution blur." />

<Warning title="the σ of the substrate belongs to the buried interface">In X-Ray Calc the roughness of the film/substrate interface is the σ field of the substrate. The σ field of the film is the roughness of its top surface. If you read the film's σ as "the roughness of the film" and the substrate's σ as a property of the bare substrate, you'll assign each fitted value to the wrong boundary. The program shows no warning.</Warning>

## What this means when you fit

The positions of the fringes fix the thickness. For the film of <a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a> they give 316.8 Å, about 1 % above the fitted total. The depth of the fringes depends on the density of the film and on the roughness of both boundaries at the same time. A light film on a substrate of similar δ gives weak fringes. They cannot separate these effects. For such a film the fitting procedure reports the thickness only. It gives σ and ρ as undetermined. Of the film in <a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a> the methods paper says: "The film yields its thickness, and nothing further."

So set the starting thickness of a film from its fringe spacing, measured far above the edge. <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch12-building-the-starting-model#the-periodic-stack`}>Chapter 12: The periodic stack</a> shows where this value goes.

A one-layer model may not reproduce the critical edge or the fringe contrast of a light film. Neither the resolution nor the substrate roughness is the cause, so don't try to repair it with them. For the carbon film of <a href="#fig-5-2">Figure 5-2</a>, no model reproduced both. <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch7-roughness-interlayers-dirty-surface#the-dirty-surface-a-contamination-layer`}>Chapter 7: The dirty surface: a contamination layer</a> describes the layer that every surface carries in air. <a href={`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/ch21-single-films#fitting-the-carbon-film`}>Chapter 21: Fitting the carbon film</a> fits this film and reports it.
