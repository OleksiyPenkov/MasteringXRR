Part 2 · Chapter 4

X-Rays Meet a Surface

In This Chapter

  • Understanding why the refractive index for X-rays is just below 1
  • Finding δ and β from the Henke tables and the density
  • Knowing the critical angle and the plateau
  • Understanding why the reflectivity falls so steeply above the critical angle

Chapter 1: The four features of a curve named the plateau and the critical edge. Both come from one fact: for X-rays, the refractive index of every material is slightly less than 1. This chapter explains that fact, and where X-Ray Calc gets the numbers behind it. The formulas use θ, the grazing angle. The X-Ray Calc chart shows 2θ, so every angle you would read off the chart is also given as 2θ.

A refractive index just below 1

The refractive index n describes how a material bends a wave and how it weakens it. For X-rays it is a complex number:

n=1−δ+iβn = 1 - \delta + i\betan=1−δ+iβ

δ is the refractive decrement. It sets how much the beam bends at the surface. β is the absorption index. It sets how fast the beam weakens inside the material. Both are small numbers with no unit. For silicon at the Cu Kα wavelength, λ = 1.5406 Å, δ is 7.61 × 10⁻⁶ and β is 1.73 × 10⁻⁷ (Table 4-1). So n differs from 1 only in the sixth decimal place. This book gives every length in ångströms, and 10 Å = 1 nm. So the wavelength is 0.15406 nm.

The index is below 1 because the electrons of the atoms respond to X-rays as if they were free. The photon energy is far above the binding energy of most electrons in the atom. Spiller adds the wave scattered by a thin sheet of free electrons to the incident wave. The sum looks like a wave that has passed through a slab with an index slightly below 1. The free-electron model of a metal gives the same result.

An index below 1 has a visible consequence. A beam that enters the material bends toward the surface, so its angle to the surface is smaller inside than outside (Figure 4-1, panel A). If θ is small enough, no angle inside is possible at all. The whole beam is then reflected (panel B). The section The critical angle and the plateau finds that angle.

Remember

For X-rays, vacuum has the higher refractive index, and every material has a lower one. So total reflection happens when the beam arrives from the vacuum side. It is called total external reflection.

Where δ and β come from

δ and β depend on two things: which atoms the material holds, and how many of them fill each unit of volume:

δ=reλ22π ρNAA f1β=reλ22π ρNAA f2\delta = \frac{r_e \lambda^2}{2\pi}\,\frac{\rho N_A}{A}\,f_1 \qquad \beta = \frac{r_e \lambda^2}{2\pi}\,\frac{\rho N_A}{A}\,f_2δ=2πre​λ2​AρNA​​f1​β=2πre​λ2​AρNA​​f2​
  • rer_ere​ is the classical electron radius, 2.82 × 10⁻⁵ Å.
  • ρ is the density in g/cm³, A is the molar mass in g/mol, and NAN_ANA​ is the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. So ρNA/A\rho N_A/AρNA​/A is the number of atoms in each cubic centimeter.
  • f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​ are the atomic scattering factors. f1f_1f1​ describes how strongly the electrons of one atom scatter. f2f_2f2​ describes how strongly the atom absorbs. At photon energies well above the binding energies of the electrons, f1f_1f1​ comes close to the number of electrons in the atom. Silicon has 14 electrons, and its f1f_1f1​ at the Cu Kα energy is 14.30.

Three rules follow from these formulas. Each of them returns when you fit:

  • δ grows in proportion to the density. A layer 10 % less dense has a δ 10 % smaller. Heavy elements have more electrons per atom and are usually denser too. So δ of W is six times δ of Si (Table 4-1).
  • δ grows with λ². Away from absorption edges, f1f_1f1​ changes little with the photon energy. So δ follows λ². A wrong wavelength gives a wrong δ.
  • The ratio δ/β is f1/f2f_1/f_2f1​/f2​. It does not depend on the density. Light materials absorb little: δ is 627 times β for carbon and 44 times β for silicon. For tungsten δ is only 12 times β.

The Henke tables. The values of f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​ for 92 elements, from 50 to 30,000 eV, were tabulated by Henke, Gullikson and Davis (1993). The tables combine measured absorption data with theory, and f1f_1f1​ is calculated from f2f_2f2​. They smooth over the fine structure close to an absorption edge, so a value near an edge can be wrong. Cobalt at the Cu Kα energy is such a case. The Co K edge is at 7708.8 eV, 339 eV below the Cu Kα energy.

X-Ray Calc stores one Henke table for each material. The table holds f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​ against the photon energy E, the molar mass, and the bulk density. For the calculation, the program converts λ to the energy, E = 12398.6/λ eV with λ in Å. Then it reads f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​ by linear interpolation between the two nearest rows of the table. A layer with ρ = 0 is calculated with the bulk density from its table. A compound is a table of its own, such as SiO2 or B4C. Tools > Create new material … makes a new one from elements or existing materials.

In X-Ray Calc

Tools > Edit Henke table… shows the table of one material. The columns are E (eV), λ (Å), f1 and f2. The bulk density is the field ρ (g/cm³). The molar mass is the field A (g/mol). Versions before 3.9.4 label it N (a.u.). Save overwrites the table. Every project that uses the material then changes with it.

Here is δ of silicon at λ = 1.5406 Å, from the X-Ray Calc table Si. The table gives ρ = 2.332 g/cm³ and A = 28.086 g/mol. Its row at 8048.79 eV gives f1f_1f1​ = 14.297 and f2f_2f2​ = 0.32485.

  1. The number of atoms: ρNA/A\rho N_A/AρNA​/A = 2.332 × 6.02 × 10²³ / 28.086 = 5.00 × 10²² atoms per cm³. One cm³ holds 10²⁴ Å³, so this is 0.0500 atoms per Å³.
  2. The prefactor: rer_ere​λ²/2π = 2.82 × 10⁻⁵ × 1.5406² / 2π = 1.065 × 10⁻⁵ Å³.
  3. δ = 1.065 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0500 × 14.30 = 7.61 × 10⁻⁶.
  4. β = 1.065 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.0500 × 0.325 = 1.73 × 10⁻⁷.

X-Ray Calc gives δ = 7.609 × 10⁻⁶ and β = 1.729 × 10⁻⁷. Table 4-1 lists the same numbers for eight materials that often appear in multilayers and substrates.

Warning: the SiO2 table is denser than fused silica

The SiO2 table in X-Ray Calc holds crystalline quartz, with a bulk density of 2.65 g/cm³. Fused silica, a common substrate, has about 2.2 g/cm³. If you leave the substrate density at 0, X-Ray Calc calculates your fused-silica substrate with 2.65 g/cm³. Its δ is then 20 % too high, and its edge appears at 2θ = 0.475° instead of about 0.433°. No message tells you. Enter the real density of your substrate (Chapter 12: The substrate), and check the bulk density of every material you use in Tools > Edit Henke table….

This constant is reNA/πr_e N_A/\pire​NA​/π, converted to these units. So the program’s formula is the formula of this section, multiplied by 2.

The critical angle and the plateau

Snell’s law links the angle of the beam inside the material, θt\theta_tθt​, to the angle outside, θ. At grazing angles, with the absorption left out, it becomes

θt2≈θ2−2δ\theta_t^2 \approx \theta^2 - 2\deltaθt2​≈θ2−2δ

with both angles in radians. Above a certain angle, θt\theta_tθt​ is real, and part of the beam enters the material. Below it, θt2\theta_t^2θt2​ would be negative, so no beam can enter. That angle is the critical angle:

θc≈2δ\theta_c \approx \sqrt{2\delta}θc​≈2δ​

For silicon, θc=2×7.61×10−6\theta_c = \sqrt{2 \times 7.61 \times 10^{-6}}θc​=2×7.61×10−6​ = 0.00390 rad = 0.224°. On the X-Ray Calc chart the edge of a bare silicon surface is at 2θ = 0.447°. δ grows with ρλ², so θc\theta_cθc​ grows with λ√ρ. A layer 20 % less dense has a critical angle about 10 % smaller. Table 4-1 gives θc\theta_cθc​ for all eight materials. Spiller lists 0.223° for Si, 0.435° for Mo and 0.555° for W at 1.54 Å. The values agree closely.

Below θc\theta_cθc​ nearly the whole beam is reflected, and the curve forms the plateau (Figure 4-2). The plateau is never quite at 1. Absorption takes a small part of the beam. The part grows as the angle comes close to θc\theta_cθc​. Absorption also rounds the edge. A material with a large δ/β has a sharp edge. At 0.9 θc\theta_cθc​ a bare silicon surface still reflects 91 % of the beam, and tungsten reflects 72 %. At θc\theta_cθc​ itself the values are 65 % and 45 %.

Which δ sets the edge of a coated sample? A real sample is rarely one material. The edge then belongs to the top part of the sample as a whole. The demo multilayer of Chapter 3: The example shows this. Take its layers without the thickness gradient: a 50 Å Si cap on 30 periods of 25 Å Si and 15 Å Mo, all smooth. The average δ of one period, δ̄, is 1.55 × 10⁻⁵, and 2δˉ\sqrt{2\bar\delta}2δˉ​ = 0.319°. X-Ray Calc puts the edge of this stack between θ = 0.30° and 0.33° (2θ = 0.60–0.66°). There its reflectivity falls from 0.72 to 0.19. The Si cap alone would put the edge at θ = 0.224°. The edge of the whole sample follows the average δ of the stack under the cap. Chapter 6: The Bragg law with refraction uses the same average in the Bragg law.

On a measured curve the plateau also falls at the smallest angles. There part of the beam misses the sample, as Chapter 1: The four features of a curve explains. Chapter 10: Is This Curve Worth Fitting? checks for it.

Why the curve falls so steeply above the critical angle

Above θc\theta_cθc​ the beam enters the material. Only a small part of it is reflected. Well above θc\theta_cθc​ the reflectivity of a smooth surface is

R≈(θc2θ)4R \approx \left(\frac{\theta_c}{2\theta}\right)^4R≈(2θθc​​)4

Here 2θ is twice the grazing angle, the number on the X-Ray Calc chart. So each doubling of the angle divides R by 2⁴ = 16. For bare silicon the formula gives 1.56 × 10⁻⁴ at 2θ = 2° and 9.75 × 10⁻⁶ at 2θ = 4°. X-Ray Calc calculates 1.64 × 10⁻⁴ and 9.86 × 10⁻⁶ (Figure 4-3). Close to θc\theta_cθc​ the formula is too low, and there you need the full calculation.

This fast fall is the reason an XRR curve is always plotted on a logarithmic axis. Between 2θ = 1° and 6°, the reflectivity of bare silicon falls by more than three orders of magnitude. A multilayer adds its Bragg orders on top of this fall. The fall itself remains. The measured curve in Chapter 1: Figure 1-2 spans more than five orders of magnitude.

A rough surface makes the curve fall faster than 1/θ41/\theta^41/θ4. That extra fall is where roughness shows on a curve. Chapter 7: A boundary with a width explains it.

Going Deeper: the reflection coefficient of one surface

At grazing angles the amplitude reflection coefficient of a surface, seen from vacuum, is

r=θ−θ2−2δ+2iβθ+θ2−2δ+2iβ,R=∣r∣2r = \frac{\theta - \sqrt{\theta^2 - 2\delta + 2i\beta}}{\theta + \sqrt{\theta^2 - 2\delta + 2i\beta}}, \qquad R = |r|^2r=θ+θ2−2δ+2iβ​θ−θ2−2δ+2iβ​​,R=∣r∣2

with θ in radians. It is the Fresnel formula for s-polarization, written for small angles. Below θc\theta_cθc​ the square root is nearly imaginary, so |r| is close to 1. Far above θc\theta_cθc​, the square root is close to θ − δ/θ, and r ≈ δ/(2θ²) = θc2/(4θ2)\theta_c^2/(4\theta^2)θc2​/(4θ2). Squaring gives (θc/2θ)4(\theta_c/2\theta)^4(θc​/2θ)4. For p-polarization the formula differs, but below θ ≈ 3° the result differs by less than 0.5 % (X-Ray Calc Help, Calculation → Polarization). X-Ray Calc does not use this approximation. It calculates the exact reflection of the whole structure. Chapter 8: From the substrate up: the recursion explains how.

What this means when you fit

The critical edge carries δ of the top part of your sample. δ carries its density. The edge also sits next to the plateau, the region where the measured curve is put on the reflectivity scale. So the edge must be inside the fitting range. Start the fitting range at θmax⁡\theta_{\max}θmax​, the measured maximum just below the edge. Chapter 14: Where the fitting range starts and ends sets it. After the fit, Chapter 18: The edge compares the calculated edge with the measured one.

Two more points follow from this chapter:

  • The material is never fitted. X-Ray Calc takes f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​ from the Henke table of each material. A fit can change δ and β of a layer only through its density.
  • The wavelength sets δ. δ grows with λ², so θc\theta_cθc​ grows with λ. Take the wavelength from your measurement file, as Chapter 10: The wavelength comes from the file shows.

Remember

A fit changes the optical constants of a layer only through its density ρ. The ratio δ/β is fixed by the material.
