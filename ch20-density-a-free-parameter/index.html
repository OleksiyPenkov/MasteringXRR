Part 4 · Chapter 20

Density: A Free Parameter, Not a Result

In This Chapter

  • Why a fitted density is not a property of the material
  • What the curve sees, and what trades with the density
  • A low density, read after its limit is extended
  • A density on its ceiling
  • How to report a density

Every fit returns a density for every layer, and the number looks like a material property. This chapter shows why it isn’t one, what a density can still tell you, and how to report it. It uses the fits of the two measured Co/C curves made in Chapters 14 to 19, and the fitting procedure’s tests on curves calculated from a known structure. It needs no new fit.

A free parameter, not a result

The fitting procedure is short on this point: “A fitted density is a free parameter, never a result. Do not report it as a material property, do not build a design, a rule or a deposition time on it, and do not spend fits determining it.” (§11.1) The author said it more briefly when the procedure was written down: “We don’t really care about density.”

The reason is in what the curve sees. X-Ray Calc takes the optical constants of each material from its Henke table and changes them only through the density. δ grows in proportion to ρ (Chapter 4: What this means when you fit). So the curve fixes the δ of each layer, and the fit turns it into a ρ. Several other things change the curve in a similar way:

  • The scale. The density of Co raises every order and the edge together, as a higher scale does (Chapter 13: The variants and what they move). With the scale held at a wrong anchor, the CoC4 fit put Co 0.49 g/cm³ higher (Chapter 14: Table 14-2).
  • A missing layer. Without the contamination layer, the C of the period took over its work, and its density fell from 2.00 to 1.77 g/cm³ (Chapter 19: Table 19-3).
  • Mixing at the interfaces. A metal layer with a mixed zone on each side holds less metal per angstrom. A single layer of lower density can look the same to the curve.

So a density takes up whatever the model and the data conditioning leave over. You can’t read it as the density of the material.

The runs agree, the models don’t

Chapter 16 reports a spread over five runs beside every fitted value. For a density that spread is small, and it misleads. Figure 20-1 and Table 20-1 give the Co density of CoC4 and CoC5 under every model this book fitted.

Without interlayers the runs of one model agree to 0.01–0.11 g/cm³. The models differ by much more. Holding the period or freeing it moves the Co density of CoC4 from 7.33 to 7.61 g/cm³, and that of CoC5 from 6.39 to 6.05. That is 4 to 30 times the run spread of the models. With interlayers the density of CoC5 rises by 0.8–1.3 g/cm³. Its runs then spread by about 0.6. The spread tells you how well one model fixes the density. It doesn’t tell you which model is right.

Warning: a density with a small spread

A fitted density with a spread of 0.01 g/cm³ looks measured to that precision. Change the period from held to free, and it moves by 0.3 g/cm³. The program shows the value to many digits. Nothing on the screen says how much it depends on the model.

On a known structure

The procedure was tested on curves calculated from a known Co/C and a known W/B4C multilayer, with counting noise. The fit started from a design with deposition errors and not from the truth. On those curves:

  • Co came out 0.07 ± 0.02 g/cm³ too high. That is a small bias, and it needed the scale solved in the fit. With the scale held at the correct anchor the bias was 0.165 g/cm³.
  • W came out −0.04 ± 0.68 g/cm³ off. The curve didn’t fix it. B4C came out +0.05 ± 0.04 g/cm³ off, and the carbide interlayer took the error, at +0.42 ± 0.17 g/cm³.
  • A wrong anchor for the scale, from 0.80 to 1.15 times the correct one, put up to +0.67 g/cm³ into Co with the scale held. With the scale solved, the worst error fell to +0.20 g/cm³ (Chapter 14: Letting the fit solve the scale).

A limit can move the error from one layer to another. In one of these tests the B4C ceiling was set at 2.37 g/cm³, below the true 2.40. The fit could not reach the true density, and the W layer took up the missing electron density: its density came out 0.48 g/cm³ too high.

So on a simple Co/C stack, with the scale solved, the density lands within about 0.1 g/cm³. A carbide interlayer next to a heavy metal already spoils it. And on a measured curve you don’t know which case you have.

A low density: intermixing

A low density of a thin metal layer is the one reading the procedure allows: “A low fitted density of a thin metal layer near its lower bound is a sign of intermixing at that layer’s interfaces, not a density of the material.” (§11.2) The author’s words were “it might indicate intermixing.” The reading has an order:

  1. Extend the lower limit first, as for any other parameter (Chapter 15: The near-bound rule). A value on its limit was set by the limit.
  2. Read the density of the extended fit. “The reading of it as intermixing stands on the extended fit.” (§11.2)
  3. Report it as a sign, and give no number for the mixing. Asked for a reference value of the reduced density, the author answered: “we need to search some papers, I don’t remember exactly”. The procedure gives none.

CoC5 shows the reading. Its Co layers are about 19 Å thick. With the limits of Chapter 15, the Co density ended on its lower limit of 6.00 g/cm³ in three runs of five. After the limits were extended, it came out at 6.05 g/cm³, 0.69 of the bulk density of Co, well inside the new limits (Chapter 17: Decide on the drift, not on χ²). That is the sign. Chapter 19 found the same thing another way. CoC interlayers lowered χ² by 0.10, beyond the spread of the runs, and with them the Co density rose to 6.80–7.39 g/cm³ (Chapter 19: Testing the model on CoC4 and CoC5). Both point to mixing at the interfaces of CoC5. Neither says how much.

Remember

Write “the fit suggests intermixing at the Co interfaces” in the report. Don’t write “the Co density is 6.05 g/cm³”.

A density on its ceiling

A density that ends on its ceiling is reported at the ceiling, unless you have a stated reason to raise it (§11.3). Before you read it as a dense layer, check the data. “Normalizing inside the beam-footprint region and the smoothing pass of section 5 both push a heavy layer’s fitted density up.” (§11.3) Chapter 15: The density ceiling sets the ceiling and its reason. Chapter 14: Why smoothing stays off explains the smoothing. The demo of Chapter 3: What the fit found shows the other side. Its Si ceiling was 2.40 g/cm³, above the table’s 2.332. The fit put the Si density on 2.40. The true value was 2.33.

Check the table

The ceiling is the bulk density of the material’s table. So the table must be right. Check its value before you set RMax.

In X-Ray Calc

Tools > Edit Henke table… shows the table of one material. The bulk density is the field ρ (g/cm³). The Density page under the chart draws the density of every layer as a bar, with the applied density profile over the bars.

Three cases show why:

  • SiO2 holds crystalline quartz, 2.65 g/cm³. Fused silica and glass are less dense (Chapter 12: The substrate).
  • B4C was held at 2.37 g/cm³ in an earlier table, a value typed in with no source. One laboratory’s record gives 2.52 g/cm³, and the table of X-Ray Calc 3.9.4.1030 now holds 2.52. With 2.37 as the ceiling, W took up the difference, as the tests showed.
  • A compound such as CoC has a table of its own, with a bulk density of 6.2 g/cm³. That value sets its ceiling too. The laboratory’s rule for a material with no limits of its own is 0.7 to 1.0 times the table’s bulk density (Chapter 15: Generous limits).

Densities across a series

One density from one fit says little. The same layer in a series of specimens says more. The author’s words: “It’s important to see the whole picture.”

Tip

Fit a series of specimens made the same way with the same model and the same limits. Then plot each layer’s density against the specimen. A density that jumps in one specimen points to that specimen’s fit or its measurement, before it points to the material.

Reporting a density

The report lists every density, and labels it. The procedure’s report has two places for it (§13, items 16 and 17):

  • In the fitted structure, the densities listed as “free parameter, not a result”.
  • In the list of values on or near a limit, a density on its ceiling with the reason it was not extended, and a density on its lower limit with the extension that was made.

Add the intermixing reading when it applies, with the extended limit it stands on. Chapter 23: Writing the numbers puts these items into the report.

What this means when you fit

Leave every density free, as the procedure does. A density held at a wrong value pushes the error into the next layer, as the W of the tests showed.

Don’t report a density as the density of the material. Label it “free parameter, not a result”.

Don’t trust its spread. The runs of one model agree to 0.01 g/cm³. Another model moves it by 0.3.

Extend a low density’s limit before you read it. Then read a low density of a thin metal layer as a sign of intermixing, without a number.

Check the data before you read a density on its ceiling, and check the table before you set the ceiling.
