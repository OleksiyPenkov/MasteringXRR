Part 3 · Chapter 19

When the Fit Will Not Converge

In This Chapter

  • A fit that has settled and still misses the data
  • The search, the measurement or the model
  • Adding what the model lacks instead of stretching a parameter
  • Peaks before fringes
  • Fitting in stages

A fit that fails a check of Chapter 18: Judging a Fit comes to this chapter. The chapter finds the cause before you change anything. Its examples are the two measured Co/C curves of Chapter 18. CoC4 fails its Bragg orders 7 and 8, and CoC5 fails its order 4, in every mode and in every run. Both are fitted here with one period, the period free within ±10 % (Periodic mode), five runs each. For CoC5 this is the fit Chapter 18 judged. For CoC4, Chapter 18 judged the Polynomial fit of order 1, at χ² 0.959. The one-period fit of CoC4 ends at 0.975 and fails the same two orders.

Two meanings of “the fit won’t converge”

People say a fit won’t converge in two different situations. The search hasn’t settled. The runs end at different χ² values. The Convergence page is still falling when the fit stops. A value sits on its limit, or the scale pane reads at bound. A setting fixes this. The X-Ray Calc Help lists ten things to try (Troubleshooting → Fitting does not converge). Table 19-1 gives each one with the chapter that explains it.

The search has settled, and the curve still misses. The runs agree, and every run misses the same feature. More population, more iterations or another start changes nothing, because the swarm already finds the best this model can do with this curve. The fitting procedure means this case: “when no setting brings the calculated curve onto the measured one” (§10.13).

CoC4 is of the second kind. Its five runs end at χ² = 0.9751–0.9760. All five put orders 7 and 8 at 0.28 of their measured height. The rest of this chapter is about this case.

Tip

Tell the two apart before you act. Run the fit five times (Chapter 16: Several runs, and their spread). When the χ² values spread, fix the search. When they agree and a check still fails, read on.

Two causes, and only two

The author gave the rule when the procedure was written down: “the fitting cannot be converged due to 2 reasons: measurement is bad (judgeable) or the model is too simple (this is the most common one).” The procedure adds how to judge each (§10.13). A bad measurement shows in the curve itself: too few counts, a misaligned specimen, a range that was cut short or is noisy. A model that is too simple shows as a feature that no value of the existing parameters can match. The model is the common cause, so test it first. The model is too simple says what to add. Testing the model on CoC4 and CoC5 does it for the two curves. The measurement: too few counts takes up the other cause.

The model is too simple

A model that is too simple is fixed “by adding what is missing (a light surface layer of contamination, an interlayer at an interface, a drift through the stack), not by pushing a parameter that stands in for it” (§10.13). Each addition moves its own features of the curve. Table 19-2 pairs each one with the misfit it cures.

Read the misfit on the residual strip under the chart (Result > Residual strip; Chapter 18: The residual by band). The strip shows misfits that the log-scale curves hide. A stretch above or below zero between two orders is a level that is off. A narrow spike at an order is a peak height that is off.

A parameter that stands in is the sign of a missing layer: “a roughness or a density that absorbs an unmatched feature is a sign of the second cause” (§10.13). Look for a σ or a ρ on its limit, or far from what the material can have, that moves when you add the missing layer. Chapter 17 showed one on CoC5. With the limits of Chapter 15, the Co density ended on its lower limit of 6.00 g/cm³ in three runs of five. Every run showed a drift of +0.20 to +0.31 Å. With the limits widened, the drift was gone. The density on its limit had made a drift look certain (Chapter 17: Decide on the drift, not on χ²).

Warning: a σ that hides a layer

A large σ can lower χ² by smoothing over a feature that a layer would explain. The fit reports the σ like any other value. Check every σ against its limit and against the layer’s thickness before you trust it.

The contamination layer: where the rule came from

The rule comes from a real case. The expert’s CoC4 fit and a test fit placed the peaks and the period alike. They disagreed between the orders. The expert’s ruling was: “In this case I select peaks. Non matching fringes can mean something on the surface (light hydrocarbons) we omit in our model. I think you can try to check this hypothesis.” A carbon layer on top lowered χ² from 7.94 to 6.09. Nothing in the period stack moved (Chapter 7: Figure 7-4). When the consequence was put to the author, that the light surface layer joins the standard starting model, the answer was “Right.” That is why the CoC4 model of Chapter 12: The top of the stack has the layer from the start.

The same test on the fit of this book shows what a stand-in looks like. Table 19-3 compares the one-period fit of CoC4 with the same fit without the contamination layer, five runs each.

Peaks before fringes

When the peaks and the fringes between them disagree, the peaks decide: “the peaks carry the period structure, and an unmatched fringe field is the first place to suspect a missing surface layer” (§10.13). This is the first rule of Chapter 13: Two rules for reading a curve, used as a verdict. A fit whose orders pass and whose fringes miss can pass. Chapter 18 reports the fringe contrast and sets no limit on it (Chapter 18: The fringes).

Testing the model on CoC4 and CoC5

The contamination layer is already in both models. Chapter 17 tested a drift: CoC4 drifts by −0.39 Å, CoC5 is uniform, and both still fail the same orders at every order of the profile (Chapter 18: When an order fails). That leaves the interlayer.

The test puts a CoC interlayer at each interface of the period, as in Chapter 7: Interlayers. The period then reads, from the surface down: C, CoC where C grew on Co, Co, and CoC where Co grew on C. Each assignment of Chapter 18: Asymmetric interlayers was fitted five times:

  • Wider under C: the upper interlayer 4–15 Å, the lower one 0.1–4 Å.
  • Wider under Co: the other way round.
  • Equal: both frozen at one H. X-Ray Calc can’t tie two layers during a fit, so the equal case was fitted at H = 1, 2, 4 and 6 Å. The H with the lowest χ² is shown.

Everything else is as in Chapter 18. The interlayers have σ = 1–8 Å and ρ = 4.34–6.2 g/cm³. That is 0.7 to 1.0 times the bulk density of the CoC table, the rule for a material with no limits of its own (Chapter 15: Generous limits). The Hmin of C and of Co went down by 8 Å, to leave room for them. The near-bound rule of Chapter 15 then ran for two rounds. Figure 19-1 and Table 19-4 give the result.

CoC5: the model lacks something, and the curve doesn’t say what. Every run of the three models with interlayers in Table 19-4 ends below the best run without them. The best χ² falls by 0.10–0.11. Order 4 rises to 0.63–0.78 and passes in 4 of the 15 runs. The three assignments end within 0.010 of each other, inside their spreads, so the curve doesn’t choose a side (§10.10). The equal scan changes little: 0.573–0.603 from 1 to 6 Å. And the Co thickness spreads by 1.0–2.7 Å. So something at the interfaces is missing. Its width and its side are undetermined.

Warning: a lower χ² with less known

An extra layer almost always lowers χ², because it adds free parameters. Look at the spread of the other parameters when you add it. On CoC5 the interlayers lowered χ². Without them, the Co thickness spread by 0.2 Å over the runs. With them, it spread by 1.0–2.7 Å. The program gives no sign of this. Only the runs show it.

The measurement: too few counts

The assessment of Chapter 10: When not to fit catches a measurement that can’t carry any fit: a cut plateau, too few points per fringe, a scan that starts too late. A curve that passes it can still be too weak in places. The high orders are the usual place.

A detector counts photons, and photons arrive at random. So the count at one angle is random too. A point of NNN counts scatters about its true value by about N\sqrt{N}N​ counts. Its relative scatter is 1/N1/\sqrt{N}1/N​: 10 % at 100 counts, 25 % at 16 counts, 50 % at 4 counts.

Going Deeper: why the scatter is √N

Divide the counting time into mmm short slices, so short that a slice holds one photon or none. Let ppp be the chance that a slice holds one. The photons arrive independently, so the slices are independent. Each slice adds 1 to the count with chance ppp and 0 otherwise. Its mean is ppp, and its variance is p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p). Means and variances of independent parts add:

Nˉ=m p,var⁡N=m p (1−p)\bar N = m\,p, \qquad \operatorname{var} N = m\,p\,(1-p)Nˉ=mp,varN=mp(1−p)

As the slices get shorter, ppp goes to 0 at a fixed Nˉ\bar NNˉ, and the variance goes to m p=Nˉm\,p = \bar Nmp=Nˉ. The standard deviation of the count is Nˉ\sqrt{\bar N}Nˉ​. This is the Poisson distribution. The chance of exactly kkk counts is e−Nˉ Nˉk/k!e^{-\bar N}\,\bar N^{k}/k!e−NˉNˉk/k!.

Apply it to the orders that fail (Chapter 18: Figure 18-3).

CoC4, orders 7 and 8. Each is one point, of 5 and 4 counts, in a tail of 1 to 3 counts. A point of 4 counts scatters by 4=2\sqrt{4} = 24​=2 counts, which is 50 %. That is twice the half-width of the band 0.75–1.25. So the order table can neither pass nor fail the model there. The two points count as orders only because the visibility rule asks for more than 3 times a background of one count.

CoC5, order 4. This is a real peak, and the model follows its shape. On the 24 points where the model stands above half its own peak, the model reaches 9.5 counts. Most of the data there lie at 6–12 counts. The measured maximum is one point of 16 counts. The order table divides by it: 9.5 / 16 = 0.59. With the model’s own value at each of the 24 points, the chance that at least one of them reaches 16 counts by counting alone is 0.27. Summed over the 24 points, the model has 187 counts and the data 227. Their ratio is 0.83, inside the band.

So the fail of CoC4 says nothing about the model. Its peaks hold too few counts for the check. The fail of CoC5 has two parts: the single high point, and the interfaces that the model lacks (Testing the model on CoC4 and CoC5). Write the cause into the verdict. For CoC4: orders 7 and 8 fail, and their peaks hold 4–5 counts, too few to test the 25 % band. A new measurement with a longer counting time at the high angles would test them.

Remember

Before you blame the model for a failing order, look at its peak in counts. At one standard deviation, a single point tests a 25 % band only from about 16 counts up.

Fitting in stages

The X-Ray Calc Help describes a fit in passes (Fitting → Staged Fitting). Fit, hold the part that has settled, and search the rest. The fitting procedure has no such step: it frees every parameter in every fit (Chapter 15: Freezing a parameter). Staging helps the search. It doesn’t add anything to the model. So it belongs to the first meaning of “won’t converge”: a model with many parameters whose runs don’t settle. The interlayer models of CoC4, whose runs spread by up to 0.12 in χ², are such a case.

  1. Fit as usual: Calc > Auto Fitting (F7), then Run.
  2. In the structure panel, right-click the stack that has settled and choose Freeze stack. Its title then shows [frozen] (Figure 19-2).
  3. Set the weighting for the part that is left. TW χ² = 1/sqr pushes the fit toward the critical edge. The top layers show most there (Chapter 9: The weights).
  4. Choose Calc > Resume Fitting. The Fitting Limits dialog opens with the button Resume (Figure 19-3). Check the RMax of every free layer, then click Resume.
  5. Right-click the stack again and choose Thaw stack. Set TW χ² back to the weighting you judge with. Choose Calc > Resume Fitting once more, now with every parameter free.
  6. Judge the result of step 5 by Chapter 18: Judging a Fit.

Resume differs from Auto Fitting in the ways the Help lists. It moves every window that is not frozen so that the value reached sits at its center, with the width kept. Figure 19-3 shows it on the contamination layer. Its ρ window of 0.6–1.4 g/cm³ became 0.40–1.20 g/cm³, centered on 0.80. Its H window of 5–40 Å became 0–35 Å. Centered on 11.80 Å, it would start below 0, so it stops at 0. A frozen stack keeps its limits. The Convergence page goes on from the last pass, and the best χ² starts anew. The fit extensions are kept. In Polynomial mode a frozen stack keeps its profile.

Warning: a window that moved

Resume moves a density ceiling that is above the bulk density, like any other limit, and says nothing (Chapter 15: The density ceiling). Check RMax in the dialog before every Resume.

A value on its limit brings a question before the fit starts. For CoC4 it read “Warning: C: S value at lower limit” and “Proceed anyway?”. It named the σ of the contamination layer, which sits at 0. The near-bound rule leaves a σ at 0 alone (Chapter 15: The near-bound rule). Answer Yes for such a value.

Compare the passes by the plain χ² of the Chart Info bar. A new weighting changes the sum that is minimized, so the weighted χ² of the two passes are not on one scale (Chapter 9: The weights). Table 19-5 gives a staged fit of CoC4 in the program.

Remember

A frozen value is your input. It is not a result. Finish every staged fit with a pass in which every parameter is free, and report that pass.

When to stop

Stop adding to the model when the next addition doesn’t pass the test of Testing the model on CoC4 and CoC5. Keep an addition only when both hold:

  • It is something the specimen can have: a contamination layer, an interlayer or a drift. A layer that only lowers χ² is a stand-in, like a σ on its limit.
  • The curve fixes it. Its parameters agree over the runs (Chapter 18: What the runs don’t agree on), and it lowers χ² by more than the runs spread. A χ² difference inside that spread “has not chosen between them” (§10.10).

By this rule, CoC4 keeps the fit of Chapter 18. Its verdict names the counts. CoC5 keeps it too, for its thicknesses. Its report adds the interlayer fits beside it: each assignment with its χ² and its spread, the gain of 0.10 in χ², and the width and side undetermined (§10.10). A fail that the counts explain is a result. Report it with its cause. Don’t loosen a limit of Chapter 18 to make a fit pass. Chapter 23: A complete report for CoC4 puts the verdict into the report.

What this means when you fit

Run the fit five times first. Runs that spread need a better search. Runs that agree and still fail need a better model or a better measurement.

Test the model before you blame the measurement. Add what the specimen can have: a contamination layer, an interlayer, a drift. Fit each addition five times. Fit every interlayer assignment the same way.

Keep an addition only when the curve fixes it: its parameters agree over the runs, and its χ² gain is larger than their spread.

Look at a failing peak in counts. A peak of a few counts can’t test a 25 % band. Report the fail with that cause.

Let the peaks decide when the peaks and the fringes disagree. Suspect the surface first when the fringes miss.

Fit in stages only to help the search. Finish with every parameter free, and judge that fit by Chapter 18: Writing the verdict.
